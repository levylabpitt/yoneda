Yoneda

Save the file first. Figure out what it is later.

You make a file, and before it can go anywhere you have to say what it is: which folder, what to call it, which of today's runs it came from. You are being asked to name a thing before anyone knows what it means. So it lands as final_v3_actually.dat, and a year later nobody can tell you which sample it came from, or whether the copy here is the same bytes as the copy there.

Yoneda does it in the other order. Save the bytes now, under a name computed from the bytes themselves, so two people who grab the same file land on the same name without ever talking to each other. What it means comes later, as relationships: this scan is of that sample, this run measured that device, this figure came out of that scan. None of it has to be right the first time. A wrong link is replaced by a newer one, and the wrong link stays, as a record that the mistake happened.

Three commitments

Nothing is ever deleted. A correction is a new record that points at the old one. Your bad run stays, because how often you are wrong is itself something worth measuring.

Identity is never a description. What a thing is called is not what it is, and that is what lets you save it before you have decided what it is.

Entities are assembled, not stored. There is no master row for a sample. A sample is just the records that claim to be about it, gathered up when you ask.

The name

The mark above is よ, the symbol mathematicians use for the Yoneda embedding. The lemma behind it says a thing is completely determined by its relationships to everything else. Read as an instruction for building things: you never write down what a sample is, you record every relationship it takes part in, and that is the whole of it.

Run your own

An empty copy is yours in about twenty seconds, and Docker is the whole list of things you need. The repository lives in the lab's GitHub, so sign in as a lab member first.

Not in the lab? Aim the book at your own group: paste your DOIs and see it built around your people and materials.
